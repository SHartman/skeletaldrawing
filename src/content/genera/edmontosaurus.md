While _Edmontosaurus_ might be one of the best known hadrosaurs, it's another one of those names that has a winding historical story before it came to its modern usage. Several different generic names have been applied, starting with _Claosaurus annectens_, named by Bone Wars alumnus Charles Marsh in 1892. There were fewer hadrosaurs in the late 19th and earliest 20th century to work with than sauropods or horned dinosaurs, and so for a while researchers lumped many species into one or two genera. Thus _Claosaurus annectens_ became _Trachodon annectens_ in 1902, when John Hatcher swept more than half a dozen different taxa into _Trachodon_. 

In Canada in the early 20th century, new specimens were given the name _Edmontosaurus regalis_, although other specimens that would eventually be referred to _Edmontosaurus_ were dumped into the genus _Thespesius_. Quite a few hadrosaurs were excavated in the opening decades of the 20th century, and so in 1942 the classic Lull & Wright monograph on hadrosaurs was published, trying to make sense of all of the new data. And they dumped all of these taxa into _Anatosaurus_.

This did finally lump the modern species of _Edmontosaurus_ into one genus (_Anatosaurus annectens_, _A. regalis_, etc.), and _Anatosaurus_ would go on to be one of the most commonly portrayed uncrested hadrosaurs for decades. Work in the late 1970s and early 1980s brought back the name _Edmontosaurus_, deciding that _Anatosaurus_ was a junior synonym of the older genus name. Work done more recently helped shrink the number of valid species down to just two: _E. annectens_ and _E. regalis_. It was even suggested that perhaps the two species are actually males and females of a single species, but better stratigraphy (measuring of rock layers) has shown that _Edmontosaurus regalis_ comes from slightly older rocks than _Edmontosaurus annectens_. It's quite possible that _E. regalis_ evolved directly into _E. annectens_, although the fossil record makes this hard to test.
