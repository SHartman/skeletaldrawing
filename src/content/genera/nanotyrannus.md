The life and times of the name _Nanotyrannus_ have been fraught with changed ideas and forks in the road. The first specimen, a somewhat battered skull, was originally named as a new species of _Gorgosaurus_. This new skull was slightly younger than other _Gorgosaurus_ remains (it was found in rocks from the very end of the Cretaceous, where we also find _Tyrannosaurus rex_), but only by a couple of million years, nothing that would be particularly surprising.

In 1988 a group of researchers felt the skull (on display in the Cleveland Museum of Natural History) was too different from _Gorgosaurus_ to remain in the same genus, so they gave it a new name: _Nanotyrannus_. This went largely unchallenged for most of a decade, although with only a skull, there were lots of questions about exactly what _Nanotyrannus_ was. 

One question that vexed paleontologists was why we don't seem to find juvenile _T. rex_ specimens, but did start finding specimens that were referred to the smaller _Nanotyrannus_? That seemed to be answered in the late 1990s, when some researchers reinterpreted all _Nanotyrannus_ specimens from the latest Cretaceous as juvenile _T. rex_. This nicely answered the question of where the little rexes were, but not everyone agreed.

This remained contentious for two decades, until the publication of NCSM 40000 in 2025 - a specimen of gracile eutyrannosaur that was fully grown, but at just 6.4 m (21 feet) long was far smaller than _Tyrannosaurus_. Since it wasn't a juvenile, and shared key skull features with the original _Nanotyrannus_ skull in Cleveland, the name was resurrected, and as a bonus we now have a virtually complete _Nanotyrannus lancensis_. So there really was a smallish, fast eutyrannosaur living alongside its gigantic relative, _Tyrannosaurus rex_.

And there was one more surprise - the same 2025 paper also found that a fairly complete specimen of tyrannosaur that had been dubbed "Jane" was also _Nanotyrannus_, although the authors felt it was different enough to warrant its own species, _Nanotyrannus lethaeus_. _N. lethaeus_ also appears to have been somewhat larger than _N. lancensis_ - while both are almost identical in length, studies of Jane's bone histology show it still had some growing to do, although it was close enough to the end of the S-shaped growth curve that the authors felt confident it end up far smaller than _T. rex_.
