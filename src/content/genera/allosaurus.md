_Allosaurus_ is the most famous predatory dinosaur from the Late Jurassic of western North America. At a typical length of 7-9 m (22-29 feet) and weighing a couple of tonnes, they would have been formidable predators, although a lone animal might have struggled to tackle full-sized _Stegosaurus_, let alone the many sauropods they shared their habitats with (the Morrison Formation produces many of the most famous sauropods, including _Diplodocus_, _Brontosaurus_ and _Brachiosaurus_). 

_Allosaurus_ had its share of naming debates, although many of them have receded further in time from the present than, say, the vigorous debate over _Nanotyrannus_. Still, the original name itself was disputed for quite a while - _Allosaurus_ was named during the Late 19th century Bone Wars era, when the feuding paleontologists Cope and Marsh competed to see who could name the most fossils. As you might expect from such a silly exercise, lots of times names were applied to scrappy remains, and several similar animals were named more than once, resulting in a mess that we are still straightening out to this day. Among several names (_Creosaurus_, _Epanterias_, etc.), in 1920 it was suggested that all of them, along with _Allosaurus_, should be sunk into the much older name _Antrodemus_. 

Many books and museum displays of the mid-20th century adopted _Antrodemus_, but in 1976 Jim Madsen concluded that the single tail vertebra that _Antrodemus_ was originally named for was not diagnostic, and worse, it's not known which rock layers the _Antrodemus_ tail vertebra was collected from. With that, _Allosaurus fragilis_ became the correct name for most of the allosaurid specimens, and when it was determined that some of the specimens deserved a second species name, it was named _Allosaurus jimmadseni_ in his honor.

There are still some interesting questions out there. Fragmentary remains indicate 10+ m (over 30 feet) long individuals, some of which _might_ deserve their own species (or genus) as well. Specimens from Oklahoma have been variously named _Saurophaganax_ and _Allosaurus maximus_, although, for reasons I won't dive into here, those names have been retired in favor of _Allosaurus anax_. But the currently known _A. anax_ remains are barely enough to deserve a name, and we will need additional specimens to unravel the mystery of these larger, more easterly allosaurs.
