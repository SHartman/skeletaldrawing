Hi Scott,It is always fascinating to look back at our initial exchange here from over a decade ago. We have certainly come a long way since the early days of the 2014 hindlimb controversy, and I appreciate how diligently you follow the shifting paradigm of spinosaurid anatomy.Looking at where the science stands today in 2026, I want to address the broader picture of Spinosaurus body size and mass, which remains a massive point of contention. While our primary Moroccan neotype specimen (FSAC-KK-11888) represents a sub-adult individual around 15 metres in length, we must remember it is just a single snapshot of a population. When we factor in isolated, larger cranial fragments—such as the massive Milan snout (MSNM V4047)—and apply our updated, short-legged anatomical framework, the reality of an 18-metre maximum length is absolutely on the table.Furthermore, we need to completely overhaul how we think about Spinosaurus weight. Older, highly conservative models often squeezed this animal into an unrealistically lightweight, paper-thin profile. However, as our 3D digital workflows have advanced, it is clear that many traditional formulas heavily underestimate dinosaur mass—especially in semi-aquatic specialists.Spinosaurus wasn't just long; it was incredibly dense. The highly osteosclerotic, ballast-like bone structure we documented completely changes the volumetric equation. When you add the thick musculature required to anchor that massive, undulating paddle-tail and a deeper, broader thoracic cavity, a mature 18-metre individual would easily cross the 12 to 15 metric tonne threshold. This puts it in an entirely different weight class than any known terrestrial theropod, including Tyrannosaurus.An animal of that immense volume and specialized mass distribution simply could not function as a standard biped or a basic shoreline wader. The physics of its center of mass demands an aquatic existence. The highly specialized, paddle-like tail from 2020 proved it possessed a powerful, laterally undulating propulsion system completely unique among known theropods.The combination of a propulsive tail fin, a dense skeletal structure, and a highly sensitive, pressure-reactive neurovascular snout system points to an animal that was fundamentally tied to the water column, operating as a true river monster.Best regards,Nizar
