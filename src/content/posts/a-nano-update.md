Since fall of 2025, _Nanotyrannus_ is back. For those who don't know, _Nanotyrannus_ was long a controversial taxon; it was named in 1988, but by the mid-1990s many researchers were questioning whether the scrappy material was really a smaller tyrannosaur, or just juvenile specimens of _T. rex_, which was found in the same environments. The issue was never resolved, but the larger number of theropod workers generally felt there needed to be an "adult" _Nanotyrannus_ specimen found to resolve the issue (the type specimen was just a skull, the Jane specimen was still growing when she died, and the Bloody Mary specimen had long been in private-ownership limbo).

After a legal battle for ownership of the Bloody Mary specimen in 2018 (itself one half of the "dueling dinosaurs" specimens), a light finally formed at the end of the taxonomic tunnel when the North Carolina Museum of Natural Sciences was able to acquire the specimen in 2020. Of course there was more prep work to be done, and good science (and good science writing) takes time, but the existence of _Nanotyrannus_ was conclusively resolved last year, when Lindsay Zanno and James Napoli published on Bloody Mary. 

![A skeletal reconstruction of the Bloody Mary specimen of Nanotyrannus](/images/skeletals/nanotyrannus-lancensis-ncsm-40000-skeletal.png "The Bloody Mary specimen of Nanotyrannus lancensis"){width=45% right}

There is much more that could be written about the saga of _Nanotyrannus_, and I did in fact write more about it! If you head to the [_Nanotyrannus_ genus hub](/theropods/nanotyrannus/), I've written quite a bit more about it there, plus you get the full size comparison between it and the Jane specimen (seen in part at the top of this post). Which is also a good time to introduce the idea of genus hubs! In past versions of the website I'd either just have multiple species of the same genus in a row in a skeletal gallery, or sometimes I'd drop them into a comparison image with some text embedded in the image. I did this for the two species of [_Parasaurolophus_](/ornithischians/parasaurolophus/), for example.

But this was sort of a hack, so now genera that have multiple species, whether _Nanotyrannus_, [_Diplodocus_](/sauropods-and-kin/diplodocus/) or [_Apatosaurus_](/sauropods-and-kin/apatosaurus/), have a new type of landing hub that not only shows skeletals and links to each species, but also has a size comparison between them, data tables, and more in-depth entries about the history of discovery, which species are considered valid, etc. 

![Two skeletal drawings, showing the known elements of two different species of Parasaurolophus](/images/blog/parasaurolophus_comparison.jpg "Back in SkeletalDrawing.com 2.0 days, this comparison between different species of Parasaurolophus was as close to a 'genus hub' as I could get."){width=45% left}

It's not perfect - I haven't done skeletals of every dinosaur species (yet)! So for example _Psittacosaurus_ does not have a genus hub, despite quite a few valid species. And some hubs don't yet have full entries written (there's only so many hours in a day!), although I have a list of them I'm working through. I'm doing similar things with species where I've restored more than one specimen ([_Tyrannosaurus rex_](/theropods/tyrannosaurus-rex/), for example). Want to explore more genus hubs? When you are browsing the galleries and click on a genus, you will automatically get taken to the hub so you can disambiguate which species you want. If you are using the search page, genus hubs can be identified as they don't come up with the second (trivial) portion of the species name. Of course the species names also come up with the search tool, so feel free to jump right to a specific species if that's what you're searching for!

### What's in a name?

I also wanted to point out that I've changed the way I handle names in the galleries. In the past I often went with the names I was an advocate for (e.g. putting the _Seismosaurus_ specimen into _Diplodocus longus_, rather than _D. hallorum_). And as someone who prefers taxonomic stability over revised names, I also sometimes held onto names too long after they had been revised (e.g. _Plateosaurus engelhardti_ over _P. trossingensis_). With this overhaul I've attempted to remove my taxonomic biases; as a result, if a newly revised name makes a [prima facie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prima_facie) argument that seems to gain some traction, I'll try to switch to the current names. 

Of course this still has an inherent judgment call requirement (as all taxonomy does). For example, I've heard rumblings that not everyone thinks there are two species of _Nanotyrannus_ (and others that think Jane is a different genus, etc.). But given the importance of Zanno & Napoli (2025) to resurrecting _Nanotyrannus_, I'm going to stick with their two species until such time as rebuttal papers are published.

Likewise, so far Greg Paul and coauthors have published two papers suggesting _Tyrannosaurus rex_ be split into three species, but _so far_ it hasn't gotten much traction with other professionals (and does have a rebuttal paper), so I'm currently leaving the names out, although depending on how it persists I may at least add some discussion about it to the _T. rex_ specimen hub at some point in the future.

Regardless, I want to emphasize that taxonomy is _always_ somewhat subjective. Any biological grouping above the species level is an artificial construct made by humans so that our brains can put things into mental categories we can handle (so "felids" indicate close relatives of cats, for example). Worse, as paleontologists we are unable to test for reproductive compatibility in fossil species, so we must rely instead on various morphospecies concepts.
